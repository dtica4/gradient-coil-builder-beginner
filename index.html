Gradient coils for the 32 mT portable MRI

Roll a flat plate into a gradient coil.

Each coil starts as copper wire laid in the grooves of four flat 3D-printed plates. Wrap the plates into a tube and the wire makes a magnetic field that changes steadily from one side of the scanner to the other. That steady change is what lets the scanner tell where a signal comes from.

What a gradient coil does

The permanent magnet ring makes a strong, even field, B0 = 32 mT, pointing along the bore. Protons in that field spin at one frequency, the Larmor frequency:

f = γ · B0 = 42.58 MHz/T × 0.032 T ≈ 1.36 MHz

If every proton spins at the same frequency, the scanner hears one note and cannot tell where it came from. A gradient coil adds a small extra field that grows in a straight line across the scanner. Now each position has its own frequency, and the frequency tells you the position.

We need three coils, one per direction. Each is designed to give 1.0 mT/m for every ampere of current (written 1.0 mT/m/A). We test them at 5 A.

The field only works as a ruler if it is a straight line. We call how far it bends away from a straight line the linearity error. Our target is below 5 % over a 50 mm field of view (FOV).

Build it, step by step

0 of 9 steps checked

Tick each step when its checkpoint passes. Your ticks are kept in this browser only.

  1. Set up Python

    pyCoilGen needs numpy 1.x, which has no ready-made packages for Python 3.13, so install Python 3.12. Keep everything inside a virtual environment, so it doesn’t clash with other projects.

    py -3.12 -m venv venv
venv\Scripts\activate
pip install pycoilgen trimesh
pip install shapely cadquery-ocp

    Install FastHenry2 from the FastFieldSolvers bundle. Open the examples folder in VS Code and choose venv\Scripts\python.exe as the interpreter.

    Checkpoint: python -c "import pyCoilGen" runs without an error.

  2. Design the three coils

    Each generation script holds an arg_dict that describes one coil. The three scripts are copies of each other with four lines changed:

    ScriptDirectionRadius (m)Length (m)Regularisation
    xgrad_coil_1198mm.pyx0.05990.35100
    ygrad_coil_1262mm.pyy0.06310.35300
    zgrad_coil_1326mm.pyz0.06630.36100

    Diameter rule: radius in metres = diameter in mm ÷ 2 ÷ 1000. For 126.2 mm that gives 0.0631 m. This is the centre line of the wire; the outside of the wire sits a few mm further out. The coils nest inside each other, so each diameter must clear the coil inside it.

    The scripts ask for target_gradient_strength: 0.9, but the solver goes past it: the designs reach 1.053 (X), 1.015 (Y) and 1.282 (Z) mT/m/A, which meets the 1.0 target.

    Checkpoint: each script writes debug/<project>_final.npy and a wire-only STL such as xgrad_coil_1198mm_wire_0_x.stl.

  3. Smooth the wire path

    A smooth path has fewer kinks and reversals. It exports to CAD in seconds and cuts cleanly. Two settings control it:

    'smooth_factor': 5,                      # default 1 = off
'normal_shift_smooth_factors': [4, 6, 4],  # default [2, 3, 2]

    The first averages each point with its neighbours. The second smooths the return paths, which are lifted 3 mm outward so crossing wires don’t touch.

    Checkpoint: rerun and compare the efficiency. Too much smoothing pulls it away from the target.

  4. Summarise and export

    Each summary script loads the saved design and prints everything you need to judge it: gradient efficiency, true outer diameter, coil length, inductance (from FastHenry2), litz resistance, and voltage and power at 5 A. It also writes the wire points as TXT and the flattened path as STEP curves, all in millimetres.

    Checkpoint: the efficiency is near the target, the outer diameter clears the next coil, resistance and inductance are not 0, and the STEP line ends with write ok: True.

  5. Unwrap the path onto a flat sheet

    To cut grooves in a flat plate, the wire path on the cylinder is rolled out flat: the angle around the tube becomes a distance along the sheet.

    • X and Y are four saddle-shaped lobes. One global unwrap of the whole path works. Splitting into lobes first scrambles it.
    • Z is a double helix that winds around many times. Instead of unrolling, every turn is folded onto one circumference (π × 132.6 ≈ 416.5 mm), cut at the seams, smoothed, and exported as four quadrant STEP files.

    Where it happens in the code. The summary scripts (summ_data_smth_…) do both jobs: they unwrap the wire path and write the flat curves as a STEP file. In summ_data_smth_x1198.py (Y is the same):

    # lines 179–186: unwrap — angle around the tube becomes distance along the sheet
r = np.sqrt(x**2 + y**2)
R_nominal = np.median(r)
theta = np.arctan2(y, x)
theta_unwrapped = np.unwrap(theta)
s = R_nominal * (theta_unwrapped - theta_unwrapped[0])   # arc length
z_flat = r - R_nominal                                     # real radial standoff

# lines 188–199: convert to millimetres, keep every 5th point
# lines 210–211: write the flat curve to STEP
step_ok = export_step_curve(flat_pattern_mm_export, step_path)

    export_step_curve (lines 29–48) passes a B-spline through every point with OpenCascade’s GeomAPI_Interpolate and saves it as one curve in a STEP file. In summ_data_smth_z1326.py the Z coil is folded instead (lines 261–263, raw angle with no np.unwrap), split at the seams (lines 284–291), smoothed (lines 350–356), and written as four quadrant STEP files plus one combined file with export_step_curves (lines 371–385).

    Checkpoint: look at the preview plot before trusting the STEP files. Curves should never jump straight across the sheet.

  6. Cut the grooves in CAD

    1. Import the curves. Upload the STEP file from step 5 into Onshape. SolidWorks or another CAD tool works the same way.
    2. Make the plate. Create a flat plate under the curves, 1.6 mm thick.
    3. Cut the grooves. Use a sweep cut with a 1.3 mm circle as the profile and the imported curves as the path. The sweep follows every contour and leaves the groove in the plate.
    4. Split into plates. Split the sheet into four plates that fit the printer: 1–4 for X and Y, TL, TR, BL, BR for Z. Each plate carries its label, so the pieces can’t be mixed up.

    The groove is a round channel: a circle of diameter d swept along the wire path and cut into a plate of thickness t. The circle sits partly below the top surface, so the opening at the top is narrower than the wire.

    To be added: how the sheet is split into the four plates, and how the labels are made.

    Checkpoint: the plates open at the right size (X sheet about 375 × 360 mm), and grooves continue across every seam. Use the 3D viewer above to compare.

  7. Print the plates

    Print all twelve plates in TPU 95A, a flexible filament (we use 3D Aura, Singapore). The ready-made files are in Files.

    Why TPU 95A? At under 2 mm thick it does two jobs that pull in opposite directions. It is flexible enough to bend each flat plate into a curve, yet once the plates form a cylinder, the tube is stiff enough to stand upright and keep its round shape without deforming.

    Lay each plate flat on the bed with the grooves facing up. No supports are needed.

    Setting (Bambu Studio)Value
    PrinterBambu Lab P1S, textured PEI plate
    Nozzle0.4 mm, standard flow
    FilamentTPU 95A
    Process profile0.20mm Strength
    Layer height0.2 mm (first layer 0.2 mm)
    Wall loops6
    Top shell5 layers (1.0 mm)
    Bottom shell3 layers (0.6 mm)
    Sparse infill25 %, grid
    OrientationFlat on the bed, grooves facing up
    SupportsNone
    Print timeAbout 4 h 50 min per plate (X, Y or Z), so about 58 h for all twelve
    Filament usedAbout 67 g (23 m) for a Z plate

    The plates are designed 1.6 mm thick. At 0.2 mm per layer that is just 8 layers, and the top shell (5) plus bottom shell (3) use all of them, so the plate prints solid and the infill setting has no effect. Printed plates measure about 1.95 mm with calipers, a little thicker than the design.

    Known problem: the wire pops out of the groove

    The groove is a 1.3 mm round channel. The printer cannot build the curved top edge that would close over the wire, so the groove stays open and the wire can lift out, especially once the plate is bent.

    Fix: lock the wire in with small dabs of TPU. Take a short piece of the same TPU filament, melt it onto the plate across the wire with a soldering iron tip, and shape it with the tip so it bridges over the wire. Repeat along each groove.

    The iron tip is hot and melting TPU gives off fumes: work in a ventilated area.

    This only applies to small gradient coils like ours. On larger coils the plates can have grooves deep enough for the wire to sit inside and stay there on its own, with no dabs at all.

    Checkpoint: each plate measures about 1.95 mm, bends by hand without cracking, and the wire fits into the grooves.

  8. Wind the litz wire

    Each coil has its own separate wire: X, Y and Z are not connected to each other. Cut each wire to the design length plus 50 cm, so there is spare wire at both the start and the end.

    CoilWire path (design)Plus leadsCut length
    X13.646 m0.5 m14.15 m
    Y14.052 m0.5 m14.55 m
    Z17.057 m0.5 m17.56 m

    Wind the loops first, the crossings last

    Every coil is one continuous path, but in a few places the wire has to cross over wire already lying in a groove: for example where it leaves the centre of a loop. Choose a start and a direction so that all the loops go in first, and the sections that run on top of them go in last. The other way round, the crossing sections are in the way of every loop you wind after them.

    Wind on a cylinder for small coils

    For small coils like ours (50 mm FOV), wind the plates already bent around a cylindrical support. If you wind them flat and bend them afterwards, the wire pops out of the grooves as the plate curves. For a large bore and FOV you can wind flat and then bend, with much less risk of the wire popping out.

    Add the TPU dabs while you wind (see step 7). You can see as you go which sections want to lift, or where the print came out poorly.

    Finish the leads

    Leave the two ends separate; don’t twist them together. Solder the tip of each lead so the measuring clips make good contact during testing. We use SCS7 alloy, 0.8 mm lead-free solder, with the iron at its maximum of 400 °C: the bare tip of a litz wire is many fine copper strands, and solder doesn’t stick to it well at normal temperatures.

    Work in a ventilated area and let the tip cool before handling it.

    Time: about 30–45 minutes to wind one coil of this size.

    Checkpoint: every groove holds wire, nothing lifts when you press along the turns, and both soldered leads are free and about 50 cm long.

  9. Join, nest and hold the coils

    Join the plates. Fuse the seams between each coil’s four plates with a soldering iron tip: melt the TPU on both sides of the seam so it flows together and bonds. The same melt-and-bond method works between TPU and other printed parts.

    The white tube used for winding (step 8) is only a tool: slide it out once the coil is wound and joined.

    Nest the coils in the gradient cover. A printed PLA cover holds the three tubes concentric. It has three ring-shaped slits, one per coil, and each slit is centred on its coil’s design radius. Slide in X first (innermost), then Y, then Z (outermost). The cover keeps the coils concentric and holds them in their round shape, so nothing else is needed to fix them together.

    Bring the leads out. All the leads leave the bore through the pairs of holes in the cover: two holes per coil, one for each lead.

    The cover file is in Files.

    Checkpoint: all three coils sit fully in their slits, round and concentric, with the six leads out through the holes. Measure each coil’s resistance: it should be close to the prediction in Test.

Common problems and quick fixes
ModuleNotFoundError: pyCoilGen
VS Code is using the system Python. Run venv\Scripts\python.exe or set the interpreter.
“No Python at …”
The venv was copied from another PC. Fix the paths in venv\pyvenv.cfg, or make a new venv.
Resistance and inductance are 0
FastHenry2 is missing. Install it, then rerun the generation script.
STL “string is not a file”
CAD_filename is missing, so the STL went to images/. Add it and regenerate.
Coil is microscopic in CAD
The points were written in metres. Export in millimetres.
STEP export hangs
The path is too kinked. Turn smoothing up, and use interpolation through the points instead of a least-squares fit.

Test the coils

We map each coil’s field on a 3D grid and compare it with the pyCoilGen design. A coil passes when its gradient is close to the design and its field is close to a straight line. Measurements from 13–15 Oct 2026, at 5.0 A.

The result

All three coils produce real, straight gradients that match the pyCoilGen design and reach the 1.0 mT/m/A target: efficiency within 3.5 % of the design, and a linearity error of about 3 % against a 5 % target. The design can be used in the 32 mT portable MRI, as long as the magnet’s field is uniform enough (see What the numbers mean at 32 mT).

How we measure

  1. Mount a 3-axis gaussmeter probe on a motorised stage and move it through an 8 × 8 × 8 grid, 7 mm apart: 512 points filling a 49 mm cube at the centre of the coil.
  2. Run 5.0 A DC through one coil at a time and record the field at every point.
  3. Repeat the same scan at 0 A with the coil in place, to measure the background.
  4. For the field-of-view numbers, keep the 208 points inside the 50 mm FOV sphere. The other 304 points sit in the cube’s corners and edges.

The motor axes are not the design axes. X = motor x, Y = motor z, and Z = 200 mm − motor y, because the motor’s y runs into the bore while the design’s Z runs out of it. The field readings get the same correction. The probe’s sign is not certain, so we report gradient sizes, not directions.

How each coil is judged

1 · Remove the background

Fit a flat plane to the 0 A scan and subtract it, so only the coil’s own field is judged.

Bz,coil = Bz(5 A) − (b0 + b1x + b2y + b3z)

2 · Efficiency

Fit the best straight line (a plane in 3D) through all 512 points. Its slope is the gradient; divide by the current.

Bz = a0 + a1x + a2y + a3z
|G| = √(a1² + a2² + a3²)    efficiency = |G| ÷ 5.0 A

3 · Linearity, step by step

Average the 64 readings in each plane (planes 7 mm apart along the coil’s axis), then compare every step between planes with the average of the 7 steps. Equal steps mean a straight line.

Gstep = (B̄i+1 − B̄i) ÷ 7 mm

4 · Linearity error (RMS)

How far the 208 FOV points sit from the straight line of card 2, as a percentage of the field change across the FOV radius. Target: below 5 %.

RMS = √mean[(Bz − line)²] ÷ (|G| × 25 mm) × 100 %

Measured vs design (%) = (measured efficiency − design efficiency) ÷ design efficiency × 100.

What the numbers mean at 32 mT

The gradient turns position into frequency. With the 32 mT magnet and the measured gradients at 5.0 A:

f = γ · B0 = 42.58 MHz/T × 0.032 T ≈ 1.36 MHz

Δf = γ · G ≈ 209–275 Hz per mm

Across the 50 mm FOV that is a frequency range of about 10–14 kHz, a comfortable bandwidth at low field, and enough for about 1 mm pixels.

The one condition: magnet uniformity

With about 5 µT per mm of gradient, a B0 error of 5 µT (about 150 ppm of 32 mT) shifts the image by about 1 mm. The gradients are strong enough as long as the magnet’s B0 varies by less than about 150 ppm over the FOV, or the gradient current is raised to match. Try it with the Magnet error slider in the lab above.

Limits and next steps

Limits of this measurement

  • Probe noise of ±3.8 µT per reading sets a floor of about 3 % on the linearity error.
  • The probe’s sign is not certain, so only gradient sizes are reported, not directions.
  • The X and Z scans lost about 0.6 % of gradient per layer, likely the current dropping as the coil warmed (the Y rescan was steady).
  • The Z 5.0 A scan is from 13 Oct and its 0 A scan from 15 Oct; this is fine because the background is uniform.
  • B0 is assumed to point along the bore (Z), as in the design.

Next steps

  • Measure each coil’s resistance cold and warm, against the 0.36–0.59 Ω prediction.
  • Run the supply in constant-current mode and log the current.
  • Align the X coil in the scanner (it sat 8.5° off the motor axis on the bench).
  • Check the Z coil’s orientation against the design (lead end).
  • Measure the magnet’s B0 uniformity over the 50 mm FOV.
  • If Y must clearly exceed 1.0 mT/m/A, run it at about 5.1 A.

None of these limits changes the verdict: X and Z clear both targets by more than the measurement uncertainty, and Y’s efficiency range (0.98–1.03 mT/m/A) straddles the 1.0 target.
